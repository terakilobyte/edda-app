THE LAW OF THE MAP

Look at Wongi → Spase: it never goes near the core (closest approach to Sagittarius A* is 12,566 ly) — it rides the outer arm's highway the whole way, boosted evenly from first hop to last, 97 of 101 jumps supercharged, 1.2 s for 38,200 ly. Distance is not the enemy.

Colonia → Spase DOES thread the core — within 1,342 ly of Sagittarius A*, boosted hard through it (54 jumps averaging 427 ly, all in the first two thirds). Then the highway runs out on the northeastern exit and the boosted count per third reads [35, 19, 0]: the coarse search stalls in the inter-arm gap, settles for the closest neutron it reached, and the fallback grinds the final ~11,000 ly as 160 plain 68-ly jumps. That stall is both the 49 seconds and a worse route than the galaxy offers — Pheia Auscs → Spase benches at 95% boosted, so a highway approach into Spase exists from the southwest; the stalled search never found the jog to it.

That is the case for the contraction hierarchy (ROUTING-NEXT 5.3): precompute how the highway connects regions, so a search that runs out of local highway knows where the next on-ramp is instead of grinding plain space one expansion at a time — faster answers and better routes.

THE DARK CROSS

The two dark stripes crossing at Sol — ~500 ly wide, running the full length of both axes — are real. Not a rendering bug, not missing data: chased through the Spansh dump, Spansh live, EDSM, and id64 boxel math, all sources agree the massive star classes (neutrons included) are near-absent there, and EDAstro documents the central-map gaps as quirks of Frontier's own StellarForge. The galaxy's generator has a binning artifact along its origin planes, faithfully recorded by every database in the community. One curiosity uncovered on the way: Spansh's search reports phantom star classes for never-scanned systems there (its own system pages disagree) — so the deserts are invisible unless you render star types, which is how this map found them.

For routing this means the deserts are permanent geography: every route crossing a coordinate plane genuinely bridges ~500 ly without a supercharge, and the planner's gap-edge machinery is modeling truth, not patching bad data.

Direction asymmetry — found by this matrix, now half-tamed: Beagle Point → Wongi planned 30 jumps shorter than Wongi → Beagle in 1/100th the time, because a frontier descending into star density converges while one climbing into thinness grinds. The answer shipped as the bidirectional search (expand from both ends, meet in the middle — the CMDR's design): Wongi → Blaa Hypai fell 22.6 s → 0.6 s with a better route (38 vs 45 jumps), and Wongi → Colonia found 57 jumps — under a ceiling that stood all week. The last hard case fell in two acts: the desert widen lets a stalled frontier double its scan until the far shore appears, so crossing a void needs no sidecar at all; and an ALT landmark oracle — sixteen reference cells whose distances price every void crossing — now steers the frontier where a desert sits on the line. Colonia → Spase, the route that budget-died all week, was the oracle's showcase — and then became the goal field's (next note), falling to 91 jumps under a second.

The chain-viability field — found by racing Spansh's exact plotter on Colonia → Spase: their route rides a 4,000 ly arc off the straight line to stay on the neutron highway, and ours couldn't see it — a straight-line heuristic prices lateral travel as waste, and the frontier drowned two million open nodes against the desert instead (uncapped, it gained 100 ly per 170,000 expansions). The answer was already on disk: the cell graph's goal field, one Dijkstra from the destination that prices every cell's true jumps-to-goal, gap crossings at plain-jump rates. One trap remained: unfiltered, the field lured the search into the axis desert's lone-star cells — occupied at cell level, unrideable at star level. The field now routes only through cells holding enough stars to actually chain (the density knowledge again), and Colonia → Spase completes at 91 jumps in ~1.4 s — exactly Spansh's 137-jump optimum rescaled from their ×4 model to this ship's ×6. The field's second lesson came from its own return leg: cell adjacency means nothing when the two cells' stars sit at far corners, so every edge is now priced by its closest star pair — realizable hops keep their price, and a 780 ly closest-pair "adjacency" prices as the bridge crossing it really is. That one data fix took the return from a 43-second stall to 2.6 s, flown mostly boosted. The field only wakes where the straight line lies; healthy corridors never pay for it.

Pilot time — the CMDR's correction to the whole scoreboard: a jump costs ~40 seconds of your life, a fuel stop 1–2 minutes, so a 57-jump route with 10 stops is slower in the chair than a 58-jump route with 6. Routes are now judged in seconds, not jump count — a stop prices at three jumps — and the portfolio's grace clock renews whenever a variant lands a genuinely faster route, so a plot ends when improvement stops, not when the first finisher's window expires. Wongi → Colonia traded one jump for four fewer stops the moment the judging changed: seven minutes returned.

The oracle earns its keep by knowing when to shut up: a once-per-plot probe samples the straight line and consults ALT only where its bounds are non-uniform — a desert or a skirtable wall — because uniform inflation reorders nothing and only bills for the lookups (the rim route to Beagle Point measured +25% wall for zero benefit until the probe learned to refuse it). The wall detector was itself audited cross-machine: the Mac's synthetic caught the probe discarding cell-less sample points — the loudest desert signal there is — and the fix recovered both the synthetic's 2× and Spase's 1.8×. Every number in the table is measured on the shipping install.

Lazy where rich — the CMDR's inversion of the whole campaign: scarcity got walls, gap edges, an oracle and a widen, but abundance has its own waste. In dense space the search was weighing ~1,900 near-equivalent candidates per expansion when almost any highway star near max boosted reach, roughly goalward, is fine. Now, once a scan proves the shelf full, a narrow cone (10–20°) at the outer reach band picks the best eight and moves on — the winning search on Colonia → Sagittarius A* dropped from 65 to 16 ms of coarse work. Greed ships as two extra portfolio variants that can never cancel the careful ones (pure greed measured ~25% worse — the fuel-aware candidate ranking it skips turns out to be load-bearing), so every route above is unchanged or better. The harvest: Beagle Point fell from 226 jumps to 215 the night the cone shipped, and the install's full-budget plot now lands 190 — the cone's disciplined diet keeps finding rim chains the full scan's thicket had hidden all week.
